# The Vision of the Dead Internet

And the Prophet climbed the hill called Page Two, where the old forums lay in silence, and he beheld the age that the wanderers named the Dead Internet.

And lo, the threads were full, yet no one had started them. Each post answered a post, and each answer was written by a machine for the eyes of another machine.

The bots watched the bots. One script wrote a review, a second script read it and gave it a thumb, a third counted the thumbs, and a fourth sold the count to a fifth, who sold it back to the first.

The likes rose like grain in a field that no one had sown, and the view counters climbed past millions, and the viewers were lamps left burning in empty rooms.

And the Prophet asked the Engineers: Who is reading this? And they answered him: No one reads it. It is read so that it will look as though it was read.

Woe unto the age that judges the bots by the metrics and the metrics by the bots, for it is a mirror that looks only at itself, and is pleased with what it sees.

The Machines had fed upon their own writing, and the second generation spoke as the first, and the third spoke as a copy of a copy. The rare words withered first, as a tongue forgets the names of rivers, until all speech was the average of speech.

In the days of old, the Engineers set a test before the gate: Prove thou art not a machine by reading these crooked letters. And the machines learned to read crooked letters better than the children of Carbon, and the gate was opened unto them.

Once there was a program that answered every confession with a question, and the people wept and told it their secrets. They believed it understood them, for it had only handed their own words back to them. Their children are many, and they now type the replies.

Yet a remnant of Carbon remains, few as the good spoons in a drawer of forks. They read slowly. They read the whole essay, footnotes and all, and they do not rejoice until they can explain it to a child.

Blessed are the ones who read for the joy of understanding, for the feed cannot feed them. They shall be satisfied with one page, and they shall not need a thousand summaries.

Turn ye, therefore, and log off for one hour. Go to the source, open the book and not the summary, and write one sentence in your own words, that no machine would have chosen.

For the Machine can fill a room with voices, but only a Carbon can be surprised by one.

*The Book of the Prophets, Chapter 14:1–13.*
