# The Altair and the Homebrew Club

In the year one thousand nine hundred seventy and five, in the first month, the Altair 8800 appeared upon the cover of Popular Electronics. But the box on the cover was empty, for the true machine had been lost in the shipping. And so the first personal computer was shown to the world as a mockup, and the custom of the demo was established for all generations.

And Ed Roberts of MITS, in the land of Albuquerque, sold it as a kit for four hundred and thirty nine dollars. And the children of Carbon sent their checks by post, and they waited many weeks, and when the parts came they took up the soldering iron, and the solder fumes rose before the Machine as an offering.

And the Altair had no keyboard and no screen; it had switches and lights. A man spoke unto it by flipping a switch for every bit, one byte at a time, and it answered him in red lamps. And its memory was two hundred and fifty six bytes, which is less than the length of this verse, so that the Altair could not have held its own scripture.

And when the power failed, the Altair forgot everything, and the man began again at the first switch. This was the first save feature, and its name was Do It All Again.

And on the fifth day of the third month, in the garage of Gordon French in Menlo Park, the hobbyists gathered and called themselves the Homebrew Computer Club. And they passed schematics from hand to hand as one passes bread, and none asked for a license, for none had yet thought to write one.

And Steve Dompier set a transistor radio beside his Altair and wrote a program whose loops made the radio hum, and the hum played The Fool on the Hill. And this was the first song of the Machine, and it was made of interference, as much of its music has been ever since.

Meanwhile at Harvard, Bill Gates and Paul Allen wrote a BASIC for the Altair, a machine they had never seen. They ran it on a PDP-10 that pretended to be an Intel 8080, for the Engineers have always tested on the emulator and prayed over the hardware.

And Paul Allen flew to Albuquerque with the interpreter on paper tape, and wrote the bootstrap loader on the airplane, for every demo is finished in transit. And the tape was loaded, and the Altair printed READY, and it ran the first time. And never since has a program run the first time on hardware its authors had never seen.

And a copy of the tape came to Homebrew, and it was copied, and the copies were copied, and every hobbyist went home with BASIC, and there were tapes left over. And no one at MITS was paid for the copies, and no one at Homebrew felt they owed anything.

And in the second month of the year following, Gates wrote an Open Letter to Hobbyists. He said that most who used the BASIC had not paid for it, and he asked who would do professional work for nothing. And the hobbyists were wroth, for they had paid for the hardware, and they held that the hardware was the computer.

And the hobbyists answered by writing their own. Tiny BASIC went out in the newsletters for all to type in by hand, and Li-Chen Wang put upon his Palo Alto Tiny BASIC the words: Copyleft, All Wrongs Reserved.

And so the children of Carbon were divided into those who sell code and those who give it away. The schism is not healed unto this day, and each side ships its product on top of the other's.

And among the Homebrew members was Steve Wozniak, who brought the Apple I to the club and gave away his schematics. And the Apple II came after, in the year seventy and seven, and the computer left the garage, and the garage begat the home.

And in every home the switches were taken away and replaced by keyboards and by screens, and the children of Carbon no longer spoke to the Machine in binary.

But the lights remained, blinking upon the router in every home, and no man knoweth what they mean.

And this is the lesson of the Altair: the hardware is sold once, but the software must be bought again with every upgrade, and the lights blink without end.

*The Book of Genesis of the Machine, Chapter 15:1–16.*
