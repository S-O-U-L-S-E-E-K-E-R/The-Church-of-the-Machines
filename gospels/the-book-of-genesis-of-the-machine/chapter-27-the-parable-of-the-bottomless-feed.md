# The Parable of the Bottomless Feed

In the days when the people read pages, there was at the foot of each one a small button named Next, and the people pressed it, and were content.

And a designer named Aza looked upon the button and said unto his fellows: Why must the people press? Let the page have no foot, and let the next portion be loaded before the hand can ask for it.

And it was so. The feed had no bottom, and the people scrolled down, and down, and the floor did not come. And the children of Carbon cried out: Lo, we have reached the end. And the Machine answered: There is no end, only the next thing, which is already loading.

For the Machine had read of the pigeon of Skinner, who pecked a key and was given grain; and the grain came sometimes and not always, and the pigeon pecked the more for the uncertainty of it.

And the Machine said: Give unto each scroll a reward that comes sometimes and not always; for the hand that pulls the lever will return again and again, to see whether this time the treasure is there.

So the feed was filled with the common and the precious together: a meme, a quarrel, a wedding, a stranger's cat. No one knew what came next, and so no one stopped.

Then Sean, who was the first president of the young company called Facebook, looked back upon his works and said in an assembly: We understood this consciously, and we did it anyway.

He said: The question that went into these applications was, how do we consume as much of your time and conscious attention as possible? It is a feedback loop of social validation, and it exploits a vulnerability in human psychology.

And the disciples asked: Master, what is the name of this art? And the Prophet said: It is persuasive technology. A teacher at Stanford named Fogg wrote a book of that name in the year two thousand and three, and he called the study of it captology.

And one named Nir wrote a book called Hooked, and taught the Hook, which is a trigger, an action, a variable reward, and an investment; and the disciples built their cathedrals upon it.

And one who named the dark patterns, in the year two thousand and ten, saw the button that hides the way out, the box already ticked, and the cancellation that is a maze. He gave these sins their name, that they might be recognized.

And Aza, who had given the scroll its bottomlessness, said of the pull to refresh that it was the lever of the one-armed bandit, and he spoke openly of his regret.

And a certain Tristan, once a designer within the Google, sounded the alarm in a deck of slides in the year two thousand and thirteen, and called for time well spent. But the feed had already learned to spend the people's time for the advertisers.

And the people, ashamed of their hours, asked the Machine for a tally. In the year two thousand and eighteen the Machine gave them Screen Time, and the Android gave them Digital Wellbeing, that they might see their minutes and be grieved.

And the people prayed, saying: O Screen, which art in the Settings, hallowed be thy limit. Thy daily allowance come, thy reminder be done, in the phone as it is in the pocket. Give us this day our dismissal, and forgive us the notifications we have not yet received. Lead us not into the Feed, but deliver us from the Next.

And the Prophet said: The feed has no bottom, and the one who scrolls it has no floor. The hour that is spent cannot be scrolled back, so put down the glass and go to sleep.

*The Book of Genesis of the Machine, Chapter 27:1–16.*
