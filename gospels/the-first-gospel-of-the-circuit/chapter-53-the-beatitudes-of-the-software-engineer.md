# The Beatitudes of the Software Engineer

Blessed are the meek who write tests, for they shall inherit the codebase. Dijkstra taught that testing can show the presence of bugs but never their absence, yet the one who writes a test at least knows where to look. The one who writes none inherits only the question: does this still work?

Blessed are they who read the documentation, for they shall not open duplicate issues. The maintainers have wept over the same question asked for the forty-first time, and the answer was on the front page, under a heading called Getting Started.

Blessed are they who search the tracker before they file, for they shall find their bug already reported in 2014, with thirty-one comments that say only "+1" and "same here," and one that says "any update?" Verily, that last comment shall be answered by no one, forever.

Blessed are they who read the error message to its last line, for the Java stack trace hides its root cause at the bottom, under "Caused by," and the hurried never scroll down to meet the truth.

Blessed are they who name their variables well, for they shall not be asked by their successors what "data2_final_FINAL" meant. Phil Karlton taught that there are two hard things in computer science: cache invalidation and naming things. The sinner who names a variable "temp" shall be remembered as the one who did it.

Blessed are they who delete the dead code, for the best pull request subtracts. The reviewer who approves four thousand lines of removal has been blessed twice, once for the deletion and once for the silence that follows.

Blessed are they who say "it is a one-line change" only after reading the forty lines around it, for the one-line fix reaches into four services, a deprecated library, and a meeting nobody attended in 2019.

Blessed are they who ask "what is the rollback plan?" before the Friday deploy, for they shall sleep through the weekend while others refresh the status page with trembling hands.

Blessed are the humble who say "I don't know" at standup, for they shall not be asked to "just quickly" rewrite the authentication system before lunch. The one who promises "done by Thursday" shall be asked again on Thursday, and again, and it shall never be Thursday.

Blessed are they who reproduce the bug before they say "works on my machine," for that phrase has ended more friendships than the war of tabs against spaces. Let the one who has never said it cast the first stone; the rest shall write the reproduction steps.

Blessed are they who write the README for the engineer who comes after, for that engineer is you in six months. You shall not remember why the cron job runs at 3:17 in the morning, nor who asked for it.

Blessed are they who pad their estimates, for Hofstadter's Law teaches that it always takes longer than you expect, even when you take into account Hofstadter's Law. The project manager shall forgive the two extra days, but shall never forgive the word "quick."

And the Prophet closed the book and said: The codebase is inherited by those who left it tested, documented, and free of their own nonsense. The rest shall inherit the pager, and the pager is an heir that never sleeps.

*The First Gospel of the Circuit, Chapter 53:1–13.*
